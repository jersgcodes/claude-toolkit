# How a mark actually gets made

**Honest framing:** I cannot browse, so this is the craft as I know it rather than a survey of current
practice. It is also a fair criticism of what I have been doing — everything so far has been step 3,
repeated. The steps that turn a sketch into a mark are 5 and 6, and I skipped both.

---

## The seven tests a mark has to pass

Run these *early*, not at the end. Most marks die on 1 and 3, and finding that out after you have fallen
in love with something is expensive.

| # | Test | How |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reduction** | Render at 16px. If it muddies, it is finished — that is a favicon, a tab, an avatar, the places most people meet you |
| 2 | **One colour** | Flatten to solid black. It will be stamped, faxed, embroidered and printed one-colour eventually |
| 3 | **Silhouette** | Fill it solid with no internal detail. Still recognisable? Nike and Apple pass; most sketches do not |
| 4 | **Memory** | Show someone for five seconds, take it away, ask them to draw it. If they cannot, it is too complex |
| 5 | **Reversal** | White on the brand colour, and the mark on a photograph |
| 6 | **Rotation and mirror** | Does it accidentally become something else? Does it read as an arrow pointing the wrong way |
| 7 | **Category** | Put it beside eight competitors. Does it disappear |

## The five principles under those tests

**Distinctiveness beats description.** A logo does not have to explain the business. The swoosh is not a
shoe. Trying to say "guardrail" *and* "scout" *and* "Singapore" in one shape is the surest route to a
generic one. **A mark that means three things means none.**

**One idea, executed exactly.** Pick the single thing and commit. Everything else goes in the wordmark,
the colour, or the copy.

**Constructed, not drawn.** Durable marks are built from defined geometry: circles of stated radii, strokes
of one weight, angles from a small set, relationships expressed as ratios. This is the step that separates
a sketch from a mark, and it is the one I have not done.

**Optically corrected, not mathematically equal.** Once constructed, you break the maths on purpose:
- Circles must **overshoot** a flat edge slightly or they look small
- **Horizontal strokes** are drawn thinner than vertical ones or they look heavy
- Gaps that are numerically equal look unequal; you space by eye
- Junctions need **ink traps** or they clog at small sizes

**Designed to date badly-never.** Whatever is fashionable now is what will look old. Gradients, 3D,
overlapping transparency, and the current sparkle motif are all timestamps.

## The process, in order

1. **Brief** — what it must say, to whom, and explicitly what it must **not** say
2. **Occupancy audit** — what does the category already look like, in colour and in form
3. **Divergence** — many rough concepts, fast and ugly, no refinement
4. **Reduction** — kill anything that needs a sentence to explain
5. **Construction** — rebuild the survivors on a grid with stated geometry
6. **Optical correction** — break the maths where the eye demands it
7. **Application** — favicon, app icon, on a photo, one colour, reversed, embroidered, 3 metres wide
8. **System** — lockups, clear space, minimum size, colour variants, what is forbidden

**Where this project actually is:** 1 and 2 are done properly (the brief, and the hue-occupancy map).
3 has been done several times over. 4 is partly done. **5, 6 and 8 have not been started**, and 7 only as
a size check. That is the honest gap, and it is why the marks read as sketches.

## Clear space and minimum size

Define both as **ratios of the mark**, never in pixels, or they break at every other size.

- **Clear space:** typically the height of a defining element — the cap height of the wordmark, or the
  stroke width times four. Nothing enters that box, ever
- **Minimum size:** the smallest at which test 1 still passes. State it in mm for print and px for screen
- **Forbidden list:** do not stretch, do not recolour outside the palette, do not add effects, do not
  place on a busy photo without the solid-bar version, do not rebuild the lockup by eye

## On the wordmark

For most small brands **the wordmark does 80% of the work**, because it is the part that is legible at
every size and the part people say out loud. It deserves more time than the symbol.

- **Set it, then fix it.** Take a good face, then kern optically — a wordmark is a drawing, not a text box
- **Caps need positive tracking.** Capitals were never drawn to sit next to each other; lowercase needs
  close to none
- **Match the mark to cap height**, not to the bounding box of the word
- **Two-word names** can carry hierarchy: one word heavier, or one word in the brand colour
- **A licensed face used as-is is fine.** Modifying two or three letters is what makes it yours, and is
  much cheaper than commissioning a custom face

## What to check before committing

- Search the trademark register for your classes and jurisdiction
- Search image-wise for near-identical marks
- Check it does not carry an unintended meaning in the languages your audience reads — in Singapore that
  is at least English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil
- Check the domain and the handles

That last group is the one people skip and regret. It is also the one I cannot do for you: I cannot browse,
so registry and availability checks have to be yours.
